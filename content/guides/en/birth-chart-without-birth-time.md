Before the twentieth century almost nobody recorded the hour of a birth. A baptism entry gives you a day, sometimes "born yesterday", and that is all. The good news is that a date alone still gives you most of a chart.

## What you can still know

**The Sun sign.** The Sun stays in each sign for about a month, so the date is enough. The only doubt is on the one day when it changes sign.

**The slow planets.** Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn and the outer planets move slowly enough that their sign is almost always certain for a given day. Together they describe how a person thought, loved, worked and worried.

**The angles between planets.** Whether Mars stood opposite Saturn or Venus beside Jupiter hardly changes in a day. These angles are often the most telling part of a chart.

**The balance of elements.** Counting how many planets fall in fire, earth, air and water signs gives a fair first impression of temperament.

## What you cannot know

**The rising sign and the houses.** The rising sign changes about every two hours, so without an hour it is simply unknown. Any chart that shows one for an ancestor with no recorded hour is guessing.

**Sometimes, the Moon.** The Moon changes sign roughly every two and a half days. On most dates it stays in one sign all day. On the others, an honest chart says "either Cancer or Leo" and leaves it there.

## Three ways to narrow it down, honestly

1. **Look for the hour in other records.** Civil birth registrations often state the hour, even when the baptism does not. In Spain they usually do from 1871 onwards.
2. **Listen to family memory, but mark it as memory.** "Born at dawn" is a clue, not a fact.
3. **Read both Moons.** If the Moon changed sign that day, read both descriptions. Relatives who knew the person often recognise one at once.

## What to avoid

Do not pick midday "to have something to show". A noon chart puts the Sun at the top of the sky and invents a rising sign that looks as real as any other. It is better to show less and be right.
