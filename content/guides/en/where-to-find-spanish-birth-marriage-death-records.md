Tracing a Spanish family is mostly a matter of knowing which office wrote things down in which century. There are four places to look, and the date of the event tells you which one to try first.

## From 1871: the Civil Registry

Spain's national Civil Registry (Registro Civil) began on 1 January 1871. From that date, births, marriages and deaths were recorded by the state in every municipality. A civil birth entry is generous: it usually names the parents and all four grandparents, with their places of origin, and often the hour of birth.

Certificates are requested from the registry of the town where the event took place, or through the Ministry of Justice's online service. Recent entries are restricted to the person concerned and close relatives.

## Before 1871: the parish books

For earlier events the record is the Church's. Parishes kept books of baptisms, marriages and burials, in many places from the late sixteenth century onwards. A baptism entry gives the child's name, the parents, usually the grandparents and the godparents.

These books are held either in the parish itself or in the archive of the diocese. Many have been filmed or digitised, and a large number can be consulted free on FamilySearch. Some were lost to fire or war, so gaps are normal.

## Who lived where: the municipal register

Town halls kept lists of inhabitants, the padrón municipal, household by household. They show a whole family at one address, with ages, trades and where each person was born. That last detail is often what tells you which village to search next. They are kept in municipal archives, and some are online.

## National and regional portals

PARES, the Spanish state's archive portal, gives free access to catalogues and many digitised documents from the national archives, including military, emigration and court records. Several regions and dioceses run their own portals as well.

## Three habits that save months

1. **Use both surnames.** Spaniards carry the father's first surname followed by the mother's. Two surnames identify a couple, which makes Spanish lines unusually traceable.
2. **Work backwards one generation at a time.** Each record names the parents; use it to find the next.
3. **Write down where every fact came from.** Book, folio, date. You will need it when two men with the same name appear in the same village.
